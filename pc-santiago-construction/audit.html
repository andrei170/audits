The three fixes that matter for hiring

Most of this site is in good shape. The gaps below are exactly the sort of thing a busy contractor has had no reason to notice, and they line up with the problem Gabe described: you have more work than you can staff, and the site is not built to help you fix that.

1. Your careers page cannot take an application

You already did the hard part most contractors skip: you have a careers page, and it says the right things about culture and craftsmanship. But the only form on it is the customer quote form, asking whether someone wants a bathroom, basement or kitchen. A skilled remodeler who reads that page and wants to apply has nowhere to tell you their trade, how many years they have, or when they can start. A short application form with those fields turns the page from a brochure into a way to actually meet candidates, and it can screen for reliability and clear communication up front.

2. Nothing on the site is measuring it, so you cannot run or track a hiring ad

There is no analytics and no ad tag at all. That matters twice over. You cannot see where your customer inquiries come from, and more to the point here, there is nothing in place to run a recruitment ad against. The way you reach good local tradespeople on purpose, rather than hoping the right person walks in, is to put your hiring in front of them and measure what it costs to get a qualified applicant. None of that can start until the site can count.

3. Make the site read like a place a good tradesperson wants to work

Two smaller things add up here. The site tells Google it is a web page and an article, but not that it is a local general contractor, so it is harder to surface in the Union County map results where both customers and job-seekers look. And the business email is a free Gmail address on a site that has its own domain. A named local-contractor markup, an own-domain email, and a short owner bio with a photo all make the business read as established and worth working for, which matters when you are asking someone to leave another job for yours.

What is already working

  • A deep, well-built site. 93 pages with a page for each service, a blog, a gallery and a reviews page. Most local contractors have a fraction of this.
  • Real, specific social proof. Named customer testimonials with the client and their town, which reads far better than anonymous star counts.
  • Easy to contact. A form on every page and nine tap-to-call links. The customer side of the funnel is genuinely good.
  • You already built a careers page. That instinct is right and rare. It just needs the one thing that lets it actually take an application.
