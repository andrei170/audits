What is good about them
  • The angles are right. “It probably just needs a repair”, “cowboy roofer not calling back”, “one price in writing, no extras”. These are the real fears of someone with a leaking roof.
  • You are in the video. A face and a local accent beat any stock photo.
  • Before and after images and a real customer quote (Barbara) instead of generic claims.
Why they probably felt like they did not work
  1. Every ad was judged too fast. Several Meta ads ran for 14 or 15 hours in total, many for 2 to 6 days. Facebook spends the first few days learning who to show an ad to. Turning it off then means paying for the learning and never getting the results.
  2. Too many ads at once. Up to six or seven different ads went live in the same week, splitting a budget so thin that none of them could prove anything.
  3. Instant forms with no feedback. A Facebook form takes two taps, so it attracts curious people as well as serious ones. Without a pixel or a way to send “this one booked” back to Facebook, it keeps finding more of the curious ones.
  4. The patch was all of Lancashire. That spreads money over towns you would rather not drive to.
  5. Mixed messages. The current ad says your roof probably just needs a repair; the earlier ones sold full new roofs. Both can work, but as separate campaigns with separate forms, so you can see which one brings jobs.
  6. Google clicks landing on errors. Two of the three Google ads show page paths that return “page not found” today.
Worth tidying in the ad text

Some ads promise “no risk” and say “Lancashire’s most trusted roofers”. Claims like that can draw complaints and are hard to prove. The real customer quote and the video do the job better.

How we would run them instead
  1. Fix first: working landing pages, a Meta pixel with lead events, and every lead logged with what happened to it.
  2. Two Meta campaigns, not twenty ads: one for repairs (your video), one for new roofs (before and after). Two or three versions each, left alone for at least two weeks.
  3. A short qualifying form: postcode, type of job, and a photo, so the people who fill it in are the people who mean it.
  4. A tight radius around the towns you actually want, set on the map, not “Lancashire”.
  5. Google search on high-intent terms (“roof leak repair Blackpool”, “roofer near me”) pointing at a page that matches the search.
  6. A weekly report in plain numbers: spend, leads, cost per lead, inspections booked, jobs won, cost per job. If a campaign is not paying, you see it in a week, not after several figures.
